A conditional sentence has two halves: the *if* clause names a condition, and
the main clause says what follows from it. The tenses in the two halves move
together, and the pair of tenses is what tells the listener how real the
condition is.

<Note label="Commas">
A comma after the if clause when it comes first: *If it rains, we’ll stay in.*
No comma when the if clause comes last: *We’ll stay in if it rains.* The same
in every type.
</Note>

<Topic
  id="zero-conditional"
  title="Zero conditional"
  form="if + present simple, present simple"
  chips={["general truths", "if = when"]}
  gist="What always happens when a condition is present. Talks about things in general, not one occasion, so if can usually be replaced by when."
  hint="Zero conditional: if + present, present — for what is true every time, not on one occasion. If can be swapped for when."
>

Use it for general truths and for results that always follow when a
condition is present. The sentence is about what happens in general, not about
one particular situation.

- *If milk smells bad, I don’t drink it.*
- *If water reaches 100 degrees, it boils.*
- *If I’ve drunk, I never drive.*
- *If people are talking all the time, I can’t concentrate.*

Because it is about every time, *if* can usually be replaced by *when* without
changing the meaning:

- *Dogs can attack you when you are scared.*
- *When the weather is bad, people don’t go shopping.*

</Topic>

<Topic
  id="first-conditional"
  title="First conditional"
  form="if + present, will + infinitive"
  chips={["a real possibility", "future from now", "no will after if"]}
  gist="Something that may really happen if the condition is met. The if clause stays in the present even though it is about the future."
  hint="First conditional: if + present, will + infinitive — a real possibility in the future. Never will in the if clause: if you need me, not if you will need me."
>

Use it for things that might happen in the future if a condition is present.
We do not know whether they will happen, but they are a real possibility. The
main clause can take *will*, *going to*, an imperative or a modal.

- *If you study, you’ll pass.*
- *If he doesn’t call you, tell me immediately.*
- *If you’ve come to class, the exam is going to be easy.*
- *If you help me, I’ll have finished by the end of the month.*

<Note label="No will after if">
The if clause talks about the future but stays in the present: *I’ll help you
if you need me*, not *if you will need me*.
</Note>

<Rule id="unless-in-case" title="Unless and in case" hint="unless = if … not. in case = because something might happen; you act either way, whereas with if you act only if it happens.">

*Unless* means *if … not*:

- *I won’t go on holiday unless I save some money.* = *… if I don’t save some money.*

*In case* is about a possibility, and the action is taken either way. Compare:

<Chart
  columns={["Sentence", "Meaning"]}
  rows={[
    ["I’ll take my umbrella [in case] it rains.", "I’ll take it whether it rains or not — because it might."],
    ["I’ll take my umbrella [if] it rains.", "I’ll take it only if it rains."],
  ]}
/>

</Rule>

<Rule id="future-time-clauses" title="Future time clauses" hint="After when, as soon as, until, once, before, after, while the verb is in a present form even though it is about the future — present perfect to stress completion.">

The same rule as the if clause applies after *when*, *as soon as*, *until*,
*once*, *before*, *after* and *while*: a present form, not *will*, even though
the sentence is about the future. Present perfect stresses that the action will
be complete.

- *I’ll go to bed as soon as I finish my homework.* / *… as soon as I have finished my homework.*
- *When I am 65, I will retire.*
- *I won’t leave until you arrive.* / *… until you have arrived.*

</Rule>

<Note label="First vs zero">
The first conditional is about one particular occasion, the zero about every
occasion. *If you don’t use oil, it tastes awful* — every time. *If you don’t
use oil, it will taste awful* — this time.
</Note>

</Topic>

<Topic
  id="second-conditional"
  title="Second conditional"
  form="if + past simple, would + infinitive"
  chips={["imaginary present or future", "unlikely or impossible", "if I were you"]}
  gist="A hypothetical situation now or in the future, and its imagined result. The past tense does not mean past time — it marks the distance from reality."
  hint="Second conditional: if + past simple, would / could / might + infinitive — an imaginary present or future. The past tense marks unreality, not past time."
>

Use it for hypothetical or imaginary situations in the present or the future.
The if clause takes past simple or past continuous; the main clause takes
*would*, *could* or *might* with a simple infinitive (*do*) or a continuous one
(*be doing*).

- *If there was a fire, it would be impossible to escape.*
- *If you weren’t making so much noise, I could concentrate.*
- *I wouldn’t have a car if I lived in the city.*
- *If it weren’t for him, I might not be talking to you right now.*

<Rule id="was-were" title="Was or were" hint="After I, he, she, it the if clause takes was (informal) or were (formal) — but advice is always if I were you.">

With *be* in the if clause, *was* and *were* are both possible after *I*, *he*,
*she* and *it*. Advice, though, is always *if I were you*:

- *If he was/were rich, he wouldn’t be living in this house.*
- *If I were you, I’d call him as soon as possible.* (not *was*)

</Rule>

</Topic>

<Topic
  id="third-conditional"
  title="Third conditional"
  form="if + past perfect, would have + past participle"
  chips={["imaginary past", "too late to change"]}
  gist="A hypothetical situation in the past and the result it would have had. Both halves look back: the condition was not met and cannot be now."
  hint="Third conditional: if + past perfect, would / could / might have + past participle — an imaginary past and its imagined result."
>

Use it for hypothetical or imaginary situations in the past. The if clause
takes past perfect simple or continuous; the main clause takes *would*,
*could* or *might* with the perfect infinitive (*have done*).

- *If you had come to class more often, you would have passed the test.*
- *I wouldn’t have been late if I hadn’t overslept.*
- *He could have died if he hadn’t been wearing a helmet.*
- *If the jacket had been a bit cheaper, I might have bought it.*

<Note label="Would and could carry the result">
The modal always goes in the main clause, never after if: *if I had known*, not
*if I would have known*. Could and might soften the result into a possibility.
</Note>

</Topic>

<Topic
  id="choosing-between-them"
  title="Choosing between them"
  chips={["one condition, four readings", "real or unreal, now or then"]}
  gist="Ask two questions: is the condition real or imagined, and does it sit in the present and future or in the past. The answers pick the pair of tenses."
>

Take one condition, *it rains*, and change only how real it is and when it
sits. The type is a reading of the situation, not a grammar label.

<Chart
  labelColumn
  columns={["", "Example", "What it says"]}
  rows={[
    ["zero", "If it [rains], the grass [gets] wet.", "Always true. Every time, not one occasion."],
    ["first", "If it [rains], we[’ll stay] in.", "Real possibility this time. The future is open."],
    ["second", "If it [rained], we[’d stay] in.", "Imagined now or later. The speaker does not expect it."],
    ["third", "If it [had rained], we[’d have stayed] in.", "Imagined past. It didn’t rain, and that is settled."],
  ]}
/>

<Chart
  labelColumn
  columns={["", "If clause", "Main clause", "Real?", "When?"]}
  rows={[
    ["zero", "present simple", "present simple", "yes, always", "any time"],
    ["first", "present (simple, continuous, perfect)", "will / going to / modal / imperative", "yes, possible", "future"],
    ["second", "past simple or continuous", "would / could / might + do", "no", "present or future"],
    ["third", "past perfect simple or continuous", "would / could / might + have done", "no", "past"],
  ]}
/>

<Note label="The one trap">
The tense after if is always one step further back than the time it refers
to: present for the future, past for the present, past perfect for the past.
Will and would never follow if.
</Note>

</Topic>
