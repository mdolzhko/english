Auxiliary verbs do more than build tenses. In conversation they stand in for a
verb phrase that has already been said, which is what makes short replies,
agreement and question tags possible.

<Note label="The one rule">
Reuse the auxiliary from the sentence you are responding to. If that sentence
has none, use *do*, *does* or *did*.
</Note>

<Topic
  id="agreeing"
  title="Agreeing with so and neither"
  form="so / neither + auxiliary + subject"
  gist="Agree with a positive sentence with so and with a negative one with neither or nor. The auxiliary comes before the subject and stays positive after neither."
  hint="So / neither + auxiliary + subject — the auxiliary comes before the subject, and after neither it stays positive."
>

Use **so + auxiliary + subject** to agree with an affirmative sentence, and
**neither / nor + auxiliary + subject** to agree with a negative one. After
*neither* the auxiliary is positive — *neither* already carries the negation.

- *I love jazz.* → *So do I.*
- *Tom has already finished.* → *So has Gina.*
- *I’m not going to accept the invitation.* → *Neither am I.*
- *I can’t concentrate.* → *Nor can I.*

<Topic
  id="disagreeing"
  title="Disagreeing"
  form="(Yes, / No,) subject + auxiliary"
  gist="Flip the polarity: a positive statement gets a negative auxiliary, a negative one gets a positive."
  hint="Subject + auxiliary, with the polarity flipped from the statement you are answering."
>

Use **(Yes, / No,) + subject + auxiliary**. The auxiliary flips polarity: a
positive statement gets a negative auxiliary, and the other way round.

- *I’m not going to accept the invitation.* → *I am.*
- *I didn’t like the food.* → *I did.*
- *You will be here before midnight.* → *No, I won’t.*
- *I couldn’t find the solution, but my wife could.*

</Topic>

<Topic
  id="avoiding-repetition"
  title="Avoiding repetition"
  form="…, but I don’t / she can’t / he had"
  gist="An auxiliary stands in for a whole verb phrase instead of repeating it. With no auxiliary to reuse, do or did."
  hint="Reuse the auxiliary from the first part of the sentence; if it has none, use do or did."
>

An auxiliary can replace a whole verb phrase instead of repeating it. If the
first part has an auxiliary, reuse it; if it has none, use *do* or *did*.

- *I thought I had lost my keys, but I hadn’t.*
- *Tom can play the guitar, and Tina can’t.*
- *She has never had to face such difficulties, but I have.*
- *She shares your opinion, but I don’t.*
- *Some wanted to go on with the plan, but others didn’t.*

</Topic>

<Topic
  id="short-answers"
  title="Short answers"
  form="Yes, it is. / No, they weren’t."
  gist="Repeat the auxiliary from the question, not the main verb, and never contract it in the positive."
  hint="Repeat the auxiliary from the question, and never contract it in the positive."
>

A short answer repeats the auxiliary from the question, not the main verb — and
in the positive it is never contracted.

- *Is it OK if we stay tonight?* → *Yes, it is.* (not *Yes, it’s*)
- *Were they living in London?* → *Yes, they were.*

</Topic>

<Topic
  id="reply-questions"
  title="Reply questions"
  form="Did she? / Hasn’t he?"
  gist="Show interest or surprise by echoing the auxiliary with the same polarity as the statement."
  hint="Same auxiliary and the same polarity as the statement — do or did if it has none."
>

A reply question shows interest or surprise. It reuses the auxiliary of the
sentence before it and **keeps the same polarity**: a positive statement gets a
positive reply question, a negative one gets a negative reply question.

- *Sarah hasn’t passed the exam.* → *Hasn’t she? I thought she would pass it.*
- *I won’t be able to be there.* → *Won’t you? That’s a shame.*
- *I can find the answer without a calculator.* → *Can you? That’s amazing!*
- *She left without saying goodbye.* → *Did she? That’s very rude.*

</Topic>

<Topic
  id="emphasis"
  title="Emphasis"
  form="do / does / did + infinitive"
  gist="Stress the auxiliary to insist or contradict. Present and past simple have none, so they borrow do, does or did."
  hint="Stress the auxiliary; present and past simple have none, so add do, does or did before the infinitive."
>

To insist or to contradict, stress the auxiliary. Present simple and past simple
affirmatives have no auxiliary to stress, so *do*, *does* or *did* goes in front
of the main verb, which stays in the infinitive.

- *Why aren’t you going to the party?* → *I **am** going to the party.*
- *Sally doesn’t speak Spanish.* → *She **does** speak Spanish. She lived in Spain for two years.*
- *You didn’t say sorry.* → *I **did** say sorry, but he didn’t hear me.*

</Topic>

<Topic
  id="question-tags"
  title="Question tags"
  form="statement, auxiliary + pronoun?"
  gist="Reverse the polarity, reuse the auxiliary, and take a personal pronoun. Three tags break the pattern: aren’t I, will you, shall we."
  hint="Reverse the polarity, reuse the auxiliary, and use a personal pronoun as the subject."
>

A question tag asks for confirmation. It reverses the polarity of the statement,
reuses its auxiliary (*do* or *did* if there is none), and always takes a
personal pronoun as its subject.

- *He is from Liverpool, isn’t he?*
- *You don’t have a car, do you?*
- *Lisa went by train, didn’t she?*

Three tags break the pattern and are worth memorising on their own:

- *I am a bad person, **aren’t I**?*
- *Open the window, **will you**?* — requests
- *Let’s go to the park, **shall we**?* — suggestions
